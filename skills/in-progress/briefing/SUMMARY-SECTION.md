# The summary section

The first section carries the whole document for the reader who reads nothing else, and it is the section a reviewer sends back. Build it in this order, and keep it to **one page**: a page turn inside the summary breaks the one property it exists to have.

## The order is disclosure order, not pitch order

A spoken pitch opens on the ask. A document opens on the subject. Using pitch order in a document is the most common failure here, and it reads as a sales deck:

1. **One short orientation on what the reader already owns.** A prior brief, a diagram they have seen. Say what that material does *not* settle: that is the gap this document fills. A paragraph, never more.
2. **The proposal itself**, stated as clauses rather than prose, in the first quarter of the page. A reader should not have to travel to find the subject.
3. **The alternative**, in a sentence or two. Describe it as what it *is*, often the absence of a rule rather than a different rule.
4. **The cost, named plainly**, and what the cost buys.
5. **Cost is not spend**, when the reader counts money (see below).
6. **Why the standard is worth it here**, pointing at the section that argues it in full.
7. **Reversibility**: what closes the window, and what a late change would take.
8. **The recommendation**, with the hand-over.
9. **The one action that follows.**

## No forward reference may be load-bearing

A reader who stops at the end of the summary must already know what each option is and why one is preferred. Pointers to later sections offer *detail*; they never hold the answer. "Section 6 lists the advantages and disadvantages" is fine only when the summary has already stated the operative difference itself.

## Name the ontological object, and do not give it peers

Ask which thing is the subject and which things are downstream of it. A standing rule and the chore that rule creates are not two things being approved: one is policy, the other is its first consequence. Presenting them as peers flatters the chore and diminishes the policy, and a reader who spots the mismatch stops trusting the document.

Two habits follow:

- State the object **before** naming it. Give the rule its clauses, then say what the document will call it. A label is a reference handle, not the thing.
- **Bound the object in the same breath as stating it.** A standing rule read as a blank cheque will be refused. Every statement of it carries its limit: what has to be true before it applies again, and what routes it forecloses.

## Put the cost in the summary, and separate cost from spend

State the ongoing burden in the summary, not only in a disadvantages list further down. An author who names the running cost in the second paragraph is more credible than one who lets the reader discover it on page five.

Then separate the kinds of cost explicitly. **Labour is not spend**: a reader with an accounting background collapses the two unless the document pulls them apart, and "high maintenance" silently becomes "expensive". Say which is which, and say when one of them is zero. When money is not in play for this reader at all, leave it out: a money sentence then raises a question nobody asked.

## Say what the cost buys in the audience's own vocabulary

The payoff has to land in the register the reader already uses. For an audit-minded reader that means claims they can imagine testing:

> "No development activity has ever occurred in production" is answered by reading one log, rather than resting on every rule ever written.

## Hand the decision over in the document's own voice

One author means first person singular: *I recommend*, not *we recommend*. The organisation is *we / us / our*, not "the company" and not the name of the body being asked; the reader is inside the same organisation, not a tribunal. Keep "the company" only where the point is the institution as against any individual.

Hand over explicitly, in that same voice: *The decision is not mine to make.* Pair it with the reason it can be made: both sides are present, in full.

## Anti-patterns, each seen and corrected

| Pattern | Why it fails |
| --- | --- |
| Opening on the ask | Reads as a pitch; wrong register for a document that is also the record |
| Opening on a cold fact the reader already knows | Re-charges cognitive cost they have already paid |
| Summary that points to a later section for the operative difference | The reader who stops here leaves without the decision |
| The proposal and its chore presented as two peer approvals | Misrepresents what is being approved |
| Ongoing cost held back until the disadvantages list | Reads as a confession when the reader finds it |
| "High maintenance" without "the money is the same", to a reader who counts money | Collapses into "expensive" |
| A summary that spills onto a second page | Trim the summary; do not let it spill |
